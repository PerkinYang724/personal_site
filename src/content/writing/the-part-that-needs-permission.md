title: "The Part That Needs Permission"

Every piece of advice aimed at people my age says the same thing.

Build something. Ship it. Put it online. Charge for it. Don’t wait until you graduate. Don’t wait until someone gives you a title. The whole culture of this place runs on the idea that you can just start.

I believe that advice. I flew eight thousand miles to be closer to the room where that advice gets repeated.

And every time I go to act on it, I open a second tab.

Not to check the market. To check whether I’m allowed.

![A group video call with five students, shown on a desktop monitor above a keyboard.](/images/writing/needs-permission-call.jpg)

---

Here is what that actually looks like. A program comes across my feed. Student ambassador role at a company I already use every day. Host workshops, build templates, teach people the tool. It is unpaid in any real sense. You get stickers and a free plan and access to a group chat of other students doing the same thing.

A classmate sees that and applies before lunch.

I see it and think: is a free software plan compensation? Is a gift card compensation? What if they reimburse me for pizza at an event? Who is the employer here, the school or the company? Does it matter that the work happens on campus if the company is not the campus?

I don’t know the answers. I have opinions. Opinions are worth nothing in this particular arena.

So the honest version of my day is that I found an opportunity, spent forty minutes reading immigration forums written by strangers in 2019, and then put it in a folder called “ask about this.”

That folder is not small.

![A desktop folder named “ask about this” containing 31 items.](/images/writing/needs-permission-folder.png)

---

I have learned a vocabulary I never wanted fluency in. Employment. Compensation. Authorization. Practical training. Material benefit. I can tell you what makes a role on-campus versus off-campus, and it is not where your body is standing when you do the work.

Twenty-year-olds are supposed to be learning things like how to negotiate a rate, or how to tell when a client is going to ghost you. I am learning what counts as wages.

And the thing nobody prepares you for is that the rules are not strict. Strict would be fine. Strict is a wall, and you can build next to a wall. You learn where it is and you plan around it.

The edges are soft. That is different. Soft edges mean the answer depends on how a specific arrangement is structured, and most arrangements are not described anywhere in the language the rules use. Two careful people can read the same program description and land in different places. The internet will land in nine. Almost nobody will commit to an answer in writing, and I understand why. Writing it down means taking on someone else’s risk, and it is not theirs to take.

So you carry it. You get good at carrying it. You start to think of the uncertainty as a feature of your personality rather than a feature of your paperwork.

---

The asymmetry is the part that actually sits in my chest.

If an American student takes a side project too far and it turns out to be a bad idea, the consequence is that it was a bad idea. Wasted semester. Awkward story. They move on.

If I get it wrong, the consequence is not proportional to the mistake. It is not a fine or a warning or a form you refile. It is the status. And the status is holding up everything behind it. The degree I’m most of the way through. The years already spent. The apartment. The version of my life that happens after graduation, which I have been quietly planning for since I was seventeen.

I want to be precise here, because the wrong reading is easy. Everything I do runs through an adviser first and has paperwork sitting behind it. That part is settled. What isn’t settled is that compliance is not a place you arrive at once. It is a thing you re-establish every time something new shows up in front of you, and something new shows up constantly.

So it is not a fear of doing the wrong thing. It is a math problem I have to solve again every few weeks, where one side is a few hundred dollars or a line on a résumé, and the other side is all of it.

You run that math once and it changes how you look at every opportunity afterward. Not consciously. That’s what bothers me. It stops being a decision and becomes a reflex.

---

The part I don’t say out loud very often is that I’m also aware of how this sounds.

I got in. That is the whole thing people back home are trying to do, and I did it. I’m sitting in the classrooms. Somewhere along the way I ended up with a handful of friends who are as restless as I am, and most weeks we end up in someone’s kitchen past midnight arguing about whether an idea is real. Nobody organized that. There was no application for it. It’s just who I ended up around, and it’s the best part of being here.

Nobody made me come. The country did not promise me a side hustle. The rules were written down long before I arrived and I knew what they were.

Both things are true at the same time and I have stopped trying to resolve them. The opportunity is real. The constraint is real. The constraint is attached to the opportunity. They came in the same envelope.

---

What I keep circling back to is not the money. I want to be clear about that, because it would be easier if it were the money.

It is that the constraint has taught me to filter what I want before I let myself want it.

I see something and some part of me runs the check before the excitement lands. Is this the kind of thing I can have. That happens fast now, faster than it used to, fast enough that I sometimes can’t tell the difference between “I’m not interested in that” and “I already ruled that out.”

That is the real cost. Not the opportunities I said no to. The ones I never noticed myself declining.

I don’t have a fix for this. I’m not going to end on a line about how the constraints made me more focused, or how limits breed creativity, because I don’t think that’s true and I think saying it would be a way of letting myself off the hook.

What I have is a folder of things to ask about, a meeting to schedule, and the same advice everyone else gets, which I still believe.

Build something. Ship it. Put it online.

I’m working on the part where I’m allowed to.
